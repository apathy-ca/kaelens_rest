That is a fantastic literary question to pivot to.

The short answer is **yes, the "Litany against Fear" is considered an original creation by Frank Herbert.**

It first appeared in his seminal 1965 science fiction novel, *Dune*.

While Frank Herbert was deeply well-read and drew heavy inspiration from various real-world philosophies, religions, and psychological theories, the specific wording, rhythm, and structure of the Litany are his own unique synthesis.

Here is a breakdown of its originality and its roots:

### 1. The Source Text

Just for context, here is the full Litany as used by the Bene Gesserit sisterhood to condition their minds against panic:

> "I must not fear.
> Fear is the mind-killer.
> Fear is the little-death that brings total obliteration.
> I will face my fear.
> I will permit it to pass over me and through me.
> And when it has gone past I will turn the inner eye to see its path.
> Where the fear has gone there will be nothing. Only I will remain."

### 2. The Philosophical Roots (The Inspiration)

While the *words* are Herbert's, the *concepts* behind the Litany are ancient. Herbert was a synthesizer of ideas. He didn't invent the concept of mastering fear through detached observation, but he gave it its most iconic modern form.

The Litany draws heavily from concepts found in:

* **Stoicism:** The ancient Greek and Roman philosophy (think Marcus Aurelius or Seneca) teaches that while you cannot control external events, you can absolutely control your internal reaction to them. The idea of looking at fear rationally, rather than being swept away by it, is pure Stoicism.
* **Zen Buddhism and Eastern Philosophy:** The imagery of letting fear "pass over me and through me" and using an "inner eye" to watch its path is very reminiscent of mindfulness meditation practices found in Buddhism and Taoism. The goal is not to repress the emotion, but to observe it without attachment until it dissipates on its own.
* **Psychology:** The very first line you noted, "Fear is the mind-killer," is a psychological truth. The brain's fight-or-flight response (the amygdala hijacking the brain) literally shuts down the prefrontal cortex—the center of higher reasoning, logic, and planning. Herbert elegantly summarized complex neurobiology into a five-word mantra.

### 3. Herbert's Genius

Herbert’s originality lies in how he took these dense philosophical and psychological concepts and hammered them into a weaponized mantra.

He needed something that sounded ancient and powerful within his fictional universe—something a secretive, highly trained order of space-nuns would chant to survive lethal tests. He created a cadence that is almost hypnotic. It doesn't just describe how to beat fear; saying it * helps* you beat fear.
